# Turning attention into demos

The posts generate visits. Almost all of them land on your profile before they
land anywhere else, and most people decide there whether you are worth a reply.
This is the ten minutes with the highest return in the whole plan.

---

## 1. Your LinkedIn profile

### Headline

The default is your job title. That is the wrong thing for a feed full of your
posts about construction software.

```
Building Majal — construction & facilities operations for contractors
in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Egypt
```

Under 220 characters, says what you do and who for. A contractor's commercial
manager reads that and knows in one line whether you are relevant.

### About

```
I build Majal, an operations platform for construction and facilities
companies.

It came out of a specific frustration: contractors run their commercial
control in one place, their programme in another, and their site records in a
WhatsApp group — and then discover at closeout that the margin left months ago
and nobody could see it happen.

Majal keeps them connected. BOQ, variations, valuations and retention on one
side. Programme with a real critical path on the other. Drawings, RFIs,
submittals, inspections and daily logs attached to the job they belong to
rather than to a thread. A field app that still works when the basement has no
signal.

Arabic and English, both directions, same system — not a language pack.

Built on open source, so it does not meter your own site team. Everyone who
needs access gets it, and subcontractors get free portal accounts.

I write here about the decisions behind it — approval control, what "offline"
actually costs, why colouring a BIM model tells you nothing, and what a
recovery position really is.

If you run a contracting business and any of that sounds like your month, I am
glad to show you the system properly. majalops.com
```

### Featured section

Pin three, in this order:

1. The construction walkthrough video
2. `majalops.com/insights/approval-trails.html` — the strongest single piece
3. `majalops.com` itself

### Experience

Add **Majal Ops** as a current position — founder, or whatever title you are
comfortable with. Without it, a visitor sees six weeks of construction-software
posts from someone whose profile says something else entirely, and the
mismatch reads as odd rather than interesting.

> **One judgement call that is yours, not mine.** You work at Odoo and Majal is
> built on Odoo. Putting both on a public profile is normal for a side project
> and a problem under some employment contracts. I do not know which yours is,
> and it is worth thirty seconds of thought before you publish rather than
> after. Nothing else in this plan depends on how you decide.

## 2. When somebody comments

The posts are the easy half. Interest forms in the replies, and most people
never follow up on a comment they left.

**Answer every comment the same day.** Not with "thanks!" — with a sentence
that continues the thought. LinkedIn shows your reply to their network, so a
good reply is a second post.

Three shapes cover almost everything:

**They ask how something works.**
Answer the question. Completely, with the specific. Do not pivot to a demo —
you are establishing that you know the subject, and the offer lands much better
after you have been useful once for free.

**They describe their own version of the problem.**
This is the good one. Ask one specific follow-up: *"Is that on the variation
side or the valuation side?"* — something only a person who has done the work
would ask. That is a conversation, and conversations are what this is for.

**They disagree.**
Take it seriously and in public. A contractor telling you that Primavera does
this better is giving you their real objection for free, and handling it well
in front of their network is worth more than the original post.

## 3. Moving to a demo

Once, and only when it is earned. Never in the first reply.

After a real exchange:

```
This is easier to show than to describe. I can walk you through it in
thirty minutes on your own project structure rather than a generic
demo — would that be useful?
```

Then:

- **Send a calendar link, not "when suits you?"** Every round trip loses people.
- **Confirm the day before.** A no-show is usually a diary problem, not a
  change of heart.
- **Ask for two things beforehand:** what they run today, and one project they
  would want to see in it. Both make the demo about them and give you the
  material to set it up properly.

### The demo itself — twelve minutes, fixed order

1. Executive dashboard: commercial exposure across live projects
2. One variation end to end, BOQ through to certified value
3. Programme against baseline, critical path isolated
4. Pin an issue on a drawing, watch it become an owned record
5. The field app offline
6. Switch to Arabic

**Lead with commercial. BIM closes a deal; it does not open one.**

Then stop and ask what they would want to see next. The second half of a good
demo is the prospect driving.

> **Before any prospect gets a link**, `construction-erp/docs/client-demo.md`
> has a ten-point checklist — remove developer mode, disable outbound mail and
> WhatsApp, confirm every person and document in the data is synthetic,
> individual expiring accounts. Staging is live but I cannot tell from here
> whether it has been through that. A demo link is a first impression exactly
> once.

## 4. What not to do

- **No pricing in public, ever.** Not in a comment, not in a DM. It is
  negotiated live, by you, once you know what they run and how many projects.
- **No connection-request pitches.** A note referencing their actual work is
  fine. A pitch is how you get archived.
- **No invented proof.** No customer names, no results, no "we helped X save
  Y". Majal has no published case studies yet. The first number you invent is
  the one you get asked to substantiate in a commercial meeting.
- **No arguing with a "not interested".** Thank them and stop. These markets
  are small and people talk to each other far more than they talk to you.
